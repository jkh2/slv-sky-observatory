## 1. The 1967 Alamosa-area horse death ("Snippy" / "Lady")

**Proposed case status:** `investigating` (with a documented mundane hypothesis that has not been independently re-checked against original veterinary notes). Not `identified`, because the CSU examination is known only through later project and organizational summaries, and later accounts conflict with 1967 descriptions of the animal's name and sex. Not `unexplained_with_available_evidence` as a final label, because a professional examination was made and a mundane account was offered.

### Dates (keep these distinct)

| Kind of date | What is known | Precision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Event window | The animal was last reported seen on 7 September 1967 and found dead on 9 September 1967. | Calendar dates from 1967 organizational reporting; clock time of death is unknown. |
| Associated last-seen detail | The September–October 1967 *A.P.R.O. Bulletin* states the horse was in the ranch yard at 7:30 p.m. on 7 September. | Clock time is from that bulletin, not independently confirmed here. |
| First wide press | National and regional papers carried the story by 5 October 1967, according to later bibliographic citations (for example to the *Amarillo Globe-Times* and other 5 October editions). Those newspaper pages were not opened in this review. | Publication date circa early October 1967; exact first local headline unverified here. |
| Official investigation | University of Colorado project investigators (named in the published report as Ayer and Wadsworth) examined the scene after heavy public visitation, "a month" after the death in the project's telling, and had a veterinarian examine the carcass. The published case write-up appeared in the project's final report (commonly called the Condon Report). | Investigation: autumn 1967. Report publication: 1968–1969 (standard citation of the study; the Case 32 text itself does not carry a separate article date). |
| Later claims | By the November–December 1967 *A.P.R.O. Bulletin*, a veterinarian who took the remains was reported to have found two bullets in the carcass. Later secondary articles name that veterinarian and describe a mounted skeleton. | Bullet claim: reported by late 1967, not dated as a necropsy document in sources read here. |

Local time in September 1967 would have been Mountain Standard or Daylight as then observed; this note does not convert the 7:30 p.m. figure to UTC because the timezone assumption was not stated in the bulletin passage read.

### Location

A ranch in the San Luis Valley northeast of Alamosa, Colorado. The September–October 1967 *A.P.R.O. Bulletin* places the pasture at the ranch of the owner's brother, "about 20 miles northeast of Alamosa," with the carcass "about a quarter of a mile north of the ranch." Later local reporting associates the site with the Sangre de Cristo foothills near the Great Sand Dunes and Zapata Falls. **Exact ranch coordinates are not fixed in the sources read and are not published here.**

### Source observations (what 1967 sources reported)

Summarized from the September–October 1967 *A.P.R.O. Bulletin*, "The Snippy Case," and from the University of Colorado project's later Case 32 write-up (which is itself already a derived investigation report, but is the official contemporary scientific investigation record):

- An Appaloosa, described in the bulletin as a three-year-old **gelding** named **Snippy**, belonging to Mr. and Mrs. Berle Lewis of Alamosa, was found dead on 9 September 1967 after failing to come in on 8 September. The bulletin says the flesh from the neck up, including the head, was missing, and that the cut around the neck appeared clean.
- People at the site reported features they took to be unusual: little or no blood about the body, darkened ground marks, flattened bushes, small holes in the ground, a substance that Mrs. Lewis said burned her hand, and radiation readings. The bulletin also claimed there were no tracks in a wide area around the horse, while tracks of a panicked run were described farther from the body.
- The University of Colorado project recorded that the death had become associated in the public mind with recent UFO sightings in the area, that the owner was a correspondent for a local newspaper, and that the site had been trampled by many visitors by the time investigators arrived. The published Case 32 text does not itself demonstrate a causal link between the death and any aerial object.
- Case 32 also records that a second dead horse was examined in the same trip and that a veterinarian attributed that second death to encephalitis. That second animal is a separate observation, often omitted in later retellings.

The November–December 1967 *A.P.R.O. Bulletin* ("'Snippy' Still Not Dead Issue") reported, as news from Alamosa, that the veterinarian who obtained the remains claimed to have found two bullets in the carcass and concluded the horse died of gunshot wounds. The bulletin itself noted that it was not known whether the bullets entered before or after the carcass was found, and that this claim did not explain the condition of the head and neck.

### Derived analysis and later interpretation (not observations)

- The University of Colorado project's veterinarian (the *A.P.R.O. Bulletin* names "Dr. Robert Adams" of Colorado State University's veterinary school; later secondary case reviews say "O. R. Adams"—**the given name is unresolved here**) reported evidence suggesting a severe infection in a hind leg that could have disabled or killed the animal, a knife cut in the neck, and removal of accessible skin and flesh by magpies and other birds through that cut. Absence of nerve tissue and viscera was described as ordinary for a carcass dead for weeks. Project investigators attributed reported "exhaust marks" to a mundane cause in their account (later secondary write-ups say alkali fungus) and concluded that a forest ranger's radiation reading, when clarified, was no greater than background. **Case 32 conclusion:** "There was no evidence to support the assertion that the horse's death was associated in any way with abnormal causes."
- APRO disputed that veterinary reading, argued that a sick horse would have shown symptoms known to the owners, and wrote that the death had not been satisfactorily explained. The same bulletin explicitly said APRO did **not** claim the horse was killed by "flying saucer people."
- A lightning-strike remark is widely attributed in later press to the county sheriff. A contemporaneous statement by the sheriff was **not read** in this review; treat the lightning attribution as later/secondhand until the original remark is located.
- From the 2000s onward, books and newspapers often state that the dead animal was a **mare named Lady** and that "Snippy" was the name of her sire or a press nickname. That later identification is common, but it **conflicts with 1967 APRO wording** (gelding named Snippy). This review does not treat either naming as settled.
- Later popular accounts add details not found in the Case 32 text read here (named judges or deputies seeing lights within 24 hours, organs listed as cleanly removed, and similar). Those details are **unverified in this review** unless a 1967 source is produced.
- The mounted skeleton later passed through private hands. *Westword* (28 October 2021) reported that Judy Messoline purchased the remains for display at the UFO Watchtower near Hooper. That is a later provenance story about the bones, not new evidence about the 1967 cause of death. Some later articles also contain date and wording errors about the Watchtower itself and should not be used as a first source for 1967 facts.

### Competing explanations

| Explanation | Status in sources read | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Infection plus a neck cut, with birds scavenging the head and neck | Official 1967–69 project conclusion; disputed by APRO | Strongest documented mundane account, but the underlying vet notes were not located in this review. |
| Gunshot (two bullets later reported in the carcass) | Reported in November–December 1967 APRO; veterinarian not named in that bulletin | Compatible with infection if a wound became infected, but timing of the shots is explicitly unknown in the 1967 follow-up. |
| Lightning, then scavengers | Later attribution to the sheriff; not independently confirmed here | Case 32 does not rest on this. |
| Deliberate human mutilation, cult activity, or "acid bath" style theories | Mentioned as speculation in period commentary (APRO cites a *Denver Post* columnist's hypothesis in order to reject it) | Not supported by the veterinary project report as read. |
| UFO or its occupants as the cause of death | Popular association; APRO declined to assert it; Case 32 found no evidence of abnormal cause | An extraordinary causal claim; not established by the documents read. Aerial sightings in the valley around the same season, if any, would still be a **separate** set of observations unless a documented link is shown. |

### What is known, unknown, and what would change the assessment

**Known with relatively high confidence:** an Appaloosa died in a pasture northeast of Alamosa in early September 1967; the head and neck were stripped to bone by the time it was found; the story was widely publicized; a University of Colorado team had the carcass examined and published a mundane conclusion; APRO published a conflicting contemporaneous account.

**Unknown or unresolved:** the animal's registered name and sex; the exact time and medical cause of death; whether a neck cut was made before or after death, and by whom; whether bullets (if present) were related to the death; calibrated radiation measurements at the time of discovery; the content of any *Valley Courier* article by the owner; sheriff's contemporaneous notes; uncontaminated site photographs with dates.

**What would strengthen the mundane (infection/scavenging and/or gunshot) account:** the original CSU examination notes or photographs; a dated ballistics or necropsy report stating wound path and timing; documentation that the neck cut and avian scavenging were observed before heavy public disturbance of the site.

**What would weaken that account:** a contemporaneous veterinary examination, made before scavenging could reasonably account for the condition, that recorded intact skin over the neck and no infection or gunshot; or instrumented radiation logs showing a well-calibrated reading significantly above background that the project did not address.

**What would be required to support an extraordinary aerial-cause claim:** physical and documentary evidence that survives the existing veterinary examination and that connects a specific observed object to the death. Popular association and later folklore do not meet that standard. Finding more 1967 sighting reports in the same week would still not, by itself, identify the cause of death.

## 2. The mid-1970s cattle-mutilation reporting wave, and associated aerial and helicopter reports

**Proposed status (as a valley-wide cluster, not one case):** `investigating` / `insufficient_data` at the level of a single SLV explanation. It is well documented that ranchers and officials *reported* unusual livestock deaths in Colorado in 1975; it is **not** established, in documents read here, that those deaths had one cause, or that San Luis Valley cases were caused by unidentifiable aircraft.

### Dates and geography

National and Colorado attention peaked in 1975. This review confirmed official correspondence from August–September 1975 about Colorado reports, and later historical articles describing a statewide wave. San Luis Valley counties (including Costilla and Alamosa) appear in later investigator compilations and 1990s interviews; **contemporaneous SLV sheriff reports were not read in original form here.**

Helicopter reports in 1975 were also recorded outside the valley (for example, press catalog records of a 24 July 1975 *Gazette* item about Elbert County). Do not collapse every Colorado helicopter-and-mutilation story into the SLV.

### Source observations (documents actually read or transcribed from official letters)

- On 29 August 1975, U.S. Senator Floyd K. Haskell (Colorado) wrote to the FBI's Denver special agent in charge. A public transcription of that letter states that his office had received reports of cattle mutilations; that "at least 130 cases in Colorado alone" had been reported to local officials and the Colorado Bureau of Investigation; that the CBI had "verified that the incidents have occurred for the last two years in nine states"; and that in Morgan County there had been reports of a helicopter used in connection with carcasses, and of persons chased by a similar helicopter. The letter asked the FBI to enter the case. Transcription: https://rr0.org/time/1/9/7/5/08/29/Haskell_Rosack/ (HTTP 200). The underlying FBI Vault file also contains this correspondence (https://vault.fbi.gov/Animal%20Mutilation/Animal%20Mutilation%20Part%2001, HTTP 200). Figures in the letter are Haskell's account of reports to CBI, not an independent count performed in this review.
- On 12 September 1975, FBI Director Clarence M. Kelley replied that the described actions did not constitute a violation of federal law within FBI investigative jurisdiction. Transcription: https://rr0.org/time/1/9/7/5/09/12/Kelley_Haskell/ (HTTP 200). This is a jurisdiction decision, not a scientific finding that the reports were hoaxes or that they were genuine unusual crimes.
- A later FBI inquiry, often dated to 1979–1980 and focused on reported cases on New Mexico lands, is summarized in secondary histories as concluding that reported mutilations appeared consistent with common predators. That later New Mexico-centered close-out should **not** be treated as a completed field study of San Luis Valley 1975 cases.

### Derived analysis and later SLV-specific claims (not independently verified here)

- Christopher O'Brien and other later writers describe intense mid-1970s activity in Costilla County, including later interviews with former sheriffs and ranchers, and reports of unidentified helicopters. A 1996 *Cyberwest* article by O'Brien repeats several such accounts (https://cyberwest.com/v7alwst1/, HTTP 200). These are **1990s retellings**, often without the 1975 case file attached in the public article. They are leads, not contemporaneous proof.
- Compilations such as Thomas Adams's *Choppers and the Choppers* list specific 1975 helicopter sightings, including some attributed to a Costilla County deputy. This review did not authenticate those entries against department records.
- Veterinary and law-enforcement skeptical explanations offered then and later include predator and scavenger damage to animals that died of ordinary causes, post-mortem skin tears from bloating, and blood settling after death. Secondary summaries of Colorado State University / CBI case reviews exist, but **the primary CBI report was not read here**, and numerical summaries in later books ("95% predators," or a subset of "real" post-mortem cuts) should not be quoted as official findings until that report is obtained.
- Historian Michael Goleman, as summarized in a 2021 HISTORY.com article, has argued that the 1970s wave coincided with economic stress in the cattle industry and may reflect how ordinary losses were interpreted. That is a historical hypothesis, not an examination of a specific SLV carcass.

### What would strengthen or weaken claims

- **Strengthen a crime or unconventional-human explanation:** contemporaneous necropsies by veterinarians, with photographs, showing tool marks incompatible with scavengers, plus identified aircraft or vehicles, and chain-of-custody records.
- **Strengthen a mundane scavenger explanation:** the same necropsy standard, applied to a defined SLV series, showing typical scavenger patterns and ordinary causes of death.
- **Strengthen a link to unidentified helicopters or other aerial objects:** dated, independent witness reports or radar/aviation records placing a specific craft at a specific carcass site, not general "helicopters were seen in the county that week."
- **Current limit:** without the CBI packet and county files, SLV 1970s mutilation-plus-helicopter claims remain **insufficiently documented in this starter note**.

### Additional sources

- Adam Janos, "The Mysterious History of Cattle Mutilation," HISTORY.com, 27 April 2021 (updated 30 January 2025). https://www.history.com/articles/cattle-mutilation-1970s-skinwalker-ranch-ufos (HTTP 200). Secondary; also contains unverified 1967 embellishments that this note does not adopt.
- *The New York Times*, "11 States Baffled by Mutilation of Cattle," 30 October 1975, is a frequently cited contemporaneous national article; an automated fetch of the archive page timed out in this session and the text was not used.

## 3. Crestone and Baca Grande reporting cluster

**Proposed status:** `insufficient_data` as a single case; treat as a **geographic cluster of mostly later reports**.

Crestone and the Baca Grande, on the northwest margin of the valley at the foot of the Sangre de Cristo Range, are frequently named in investigator logs and in self-submitted report databases. This review did not find a single well-sourced, contemporaneously documented "Crestone case" comparable to the 1967 horse death or to the 1975 official correspondence.

Examples of what is publicly discussed, with caveats:

- A NUFORC report page (id 4192) is indexed as a purported September 1967 (date approximate) observation near Crestone of three fireball-like objects, **submitted in 1998**. The nuforc.org page returned HTTP 403/503 to automated fetches in this session, so the narrative was not verified from the live record. Even if confirmed, a report filed about 31 years later is not a 1967 primary source.
- Christopher O'Brien's published logs (see section 6) include many 1990s–2000s Crestone/Baca light and craft reports (for example, items later posted on his site, https://www.ourstrangeplanet.com/the-san-luis-valley/greater-san-luis-valley-event-log-of-unusual-occurences/, HTTP 200). These are investigator compilations, often single-witness or small-group reports, and should be checked back to original notes or interviews before use as case records.

**Mundane alternatives** that must be considered for lights over the Sangres include aircraft and helicopters (there is military and general aviation in the region), satellites and iridium-type flares (historical), meteors, astronomical objects, and terrestrial lights seen against mountain ridges at night. Missing flight data would not prove that no aircraft was present (evidence policy).

**What would strengthen a specific Crestone case:** multiple independent contemporaneous reports with clock times, bearings, and observer locations (approximate/public), plus a check against weather, astronomical, and permitted flight/satellite data for that interval.

## 4. Great Sand Dunes area

**Proposed status:** `insufficient_data` as a named historical "case."

The dunes sit east of the valley floor and are geographically close to both the 1967 ranch area (as later writers describe it) and the later UFO Watchtower on Colorado 17. Tourist and investigator literature often treats the dunes as a sighting backdrop. This review did not open a contemporaneous official or newspaper investigation that stands as a canonical "Great Sand Dunes UFO case."

Self-submitted reports exist in third-party indexes (for example, later NUFORC-attributed items for Great Sand Dunes National Park and nearby Mosca/Piñon Flats). Those pages were not verified from nuforc.org in this session.

The honest statement is: **the area is repeatedly named in later reporting; a well-documented, dated, multi-source event on the dunes themselves has not been established here.** Future work should search park records, *Valley Courier* archives, and NUFORC/MUFON with event/report dates distinguished.

## 5. The UFO Watchtower near Hooper (a public site, not a single case)

**Proposed status:** not a case. It is a **documented public observation platform and roadside business**, useful as context and as a possible future source of *public, consented* sighting accounts if terms allow.

### What contemporaneous and later news support

- On 7 October 1999, the *Pueblo Chieftain* reported that Saguache County commissioners had approved a conditional-use permit for Judy Messoline to build a UFO watch tower and gift shop on land north of Hooper, off Colorado 17, with a tower not more than about 10 feet high and limited overnight parking. Messoline was quoted as saying she had not seen a flying saucer, that she planned a summer-season operation aimed at tourists, and that people had already been parking on her land. Automated fetch of the article returned HTTP 402 (paywall or access control); the account above is from a search extract of that article and should be re-opened in a browser before being treated as fully verified wording. URL: https://www.chieftain.com/story/special/1999/10/07/saguache-tower-to-spy-ufos/8893953007/
- On 30 May 2000, the *Chieftain* reported the opening (with partner Stan Becker) of the UFO Watchtower a few miles north of Hooper, with camping/parking, a shop, and a view toward the Sangre de Cristo Mountains and Great Sand Dunes. Same access limitation (HTTP 402). URL: https://www.chieftain.com/story/news/2000/05/30/ufo-seekers-line-up-all/8651517007/
- *The Colorado Sun* (24 August 2023) reported, from an on-site visit and interview, that Messoline said 297 sightings had been recorded at the watchtower, that she had witnessed 30, and that one cigar-shaped object was seen by her and "10 other people one night." Those figures are **operator-reported**, not an audited catalog, in the article as read. https://coloradosun.com/2023/08/24/ufo-watchtower-congressional-hearings-mufon-colorado/ (HTTP 200).
- NPR (30 April 2025), in reporting on the site's 25th year, quoted Messoline as saying the tower began as a joke and tourist draw, that 304 sightings had been recorded there, that she had had 30 sightings, and that the closest she described as cigar-shaped. Attendance was reported as about 10,000 visitors a year. https://www.npr.org/2025/04/30/nx-s1-5381040/ufo-watchtower-in-colorado-that-started-as-a-joke-now-draws-thousands-per-year (HTTP 200). A related CPR article URL returned HTTP 403 to automated fetch.
- *Westword* (28 October 2021) reported the planned display of the 1967 horse skeleton at the site (see section 1).

### Interpretation notes

The Watchtower is evidence that the valley has a long-running public association with UFO tourism and that the operator keeps a visitor sighting binder. It is **not**, without further work, a validated data set. Individual binder entries would need consent, dates, and independent checks before becoming case records. Exact camp-site or visitor identities should not be published.

Recent (2023–2026) news about the Watchtower is mostly institutional or tourism coverage, sometimes tied to national UAP hearings, not a newly documented landmark investigation of a single SLV object.

## 6. Christopher O'Brien's SLV research (secondary literature and investigator corpus)

**Proposed status:** a **source class**, not a case.

Christopher O'Brien lived in the valley and published a trilogy of books on unusual reports there, based on work he dates largely to 1992–2002:

- *The Mysterious Valley*, St. Martin's Paperbacks, 1996 (ISBN 0-312-95883-8 / 9780312958831). Month/day listings vary in retail catalogs; the year is consistently 1996.
- *Enter the Valley*, St. Martin's Paperbacks, 1999 (ISBN 0-312-96835-3 / 9780312968359). Catalogs disagree on the exact day in early 1999; the year is 1999.
- *Secrets of the Mysterious Valley*, 2007 (ISBN 978-1-931882-66-8). Retail listings commonly give May 2007; the publisher name was not independently confirmed from a catalog page in this review and is left unspecified rather than guessed.

His own site describes a decade of collecting UFO, livestock-death, folklore, and related reports, working with law enforcement and local contacts ([About page](https://www.ourstrangeplanet.com/about-chris/), HTTP 200). *Enter the Valley*, as described in its opening (a scan is circulating online; the book itself is copyrighted and should be cited from a lawful copy), presents itself as a follow-up to the 1996 book and includes a report log.

**How this project should use this corpus:** as a rich **secondary** map of claims, names of public officials, and dates to check against primary records. It is not a substitute for those records. Counts of "over one thousand" events are the author's characterization. Anonymized or informal logs can mix observations, secondhand rumors, and interpretation; each item used in a case file should be traced, dated (event vs. interview vs. publication), and labeled.

## 8. Recent reports and news, 2020–2026

What this review actually found for the period is, overwhelmingly, **coverage of the Watchtower, valley folklore, and national UAP politics**, not a new, well-documented SLV investigation comparable to 1967 or to the 1975 official correspondence.

- 2021: *Westword* on the horse skeleton's move to the Watchtower (section 1 and 5).
- 2023: *Colorado Sun* on Watchtower traffic after congressional UAP hearings; Colorado MUFON caseload figures in that article are **statewide**, not SLV-specific.
- 2025: NPR (and related CPR, not fully opened) on the Watchtower's 25th year and operator-reported sighting counts.
- 2026: general-interest folklore features (for example, a 5 May 2026 *Denver Gazette* piece; HTTP 200) repeat the valley's reputation; they do not constitute new case investigations.
- Unverified web report posts (including hobby sighting sites in 2025) are noted only as leads. They were not used as evidence.

If a 2020–2026 SLV case is to be added, it should start from a named date, a public-quality source, and a check against ordinary traffic, astronomy, and balloons/satellites—not from tourism copy.
